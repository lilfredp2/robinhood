How it works

  1. One of you starts a room. Parlor prepares a connection and hands you an invite code — a long string of text, or a link that carries it.
  2. Send it however you like. Text, email, a note on the fridge. The code describes how to reach your browser; it is not a password and it expires when you close the tab.
  3. They paste it back. Joining produces a reply code. Paste that into the first window and the two browsers connect directly.
  4. Everything rides that one link. Messages, camera, microphone, screen and pictures all travel over the connection you just made.

No account, no inbox

There is nothing to sign up for and nowhere for your conversation to sit. The message log lives in this browser's storage and the pictures in its local database — clearing the room deletes both.

Pictures, sized for sending

Photos are scaled down to 1600px and re-encoded before they leave, then sent in 16 KB pieces with a progress bar. Originals never leave your device.

Built to be read

Plain HTML, CSS and three ES modules — no framework, no build step, no tracking. Light and dark are separately drawn, and every control is reachable from the keyboard.

Privacy, plainly

What stays on your device

Messages, the pictures you send and receive, your display name and your theme choice. Nothing is uploaded, because there is no service behind this page to upload it to.

What leaves it

Your browser asks a public STUN server for your network address so the two peers can find each other; that is the one outside request Parlor makes. Media itself is encrypted in transit by WebRTC and flows directly between you.

Where it can fail

Some strict corporate or mobile networks block direct connections. Parlor ships no relay server, so on those networks the connection will not complete — a note appears rather than a silent hang.

Who you are talking to

An invite code is only as private as the channel you send it over. Anyone holding an unused code can take the other seat, so share it the way you would share a door key.
